Layer 5 Session 12 Release Strategies

Health Probes and Autoscaling

Session 11 built three ways to flip traffic between a blue and a green environment — but every one of those approaches quietly assumed the environment you flip to is actually healthy and sized correctly for the load it's about to receive. Today closes that gap: liveness, readiness, and startup probes that let Kubernetes tell a broken pod from a working one, and a HorizontalPodAutoscaler that keeps the right number of pods running for the traffic that's actually arriving.

Estimated time: 45–55 minutes

Flow: Pre-quiz → Concept → Lab → Commit → Post-quiz

  1. 1/5Distinguish a liveness probe (restarts the container) from a readiness probe (gates traffic, no restart)

  3. 3/5Configure all three probes on a Deployment and simulate a failure to observe Kubernetes react
  4. 4/5Explain how the metrics-server feeds CPU/memory data to a HorizontalPodAutoscaler

2. Pre-Coding Quiz

Answer before reading the concept. Need 4/5 to proceed.

Question 1 of 5

What is the core difference between a liveness probe and a readiness probe?

Liveness answers "is this container stuck?" and triggers a restart on repeated failure. Readiness answers "can this pod serve a request right now?" and only affects Service endpoints.
Question 2 of 5

After a liveness probe fails three times in a row, what happens?

The kubelet, running on the node, is responsible for acting on liveness probe failures — it restarts just the container, not the whole pod's identity or the node.
Question 3 of 5

A pod's readiness probe starts failing, but its liveness probe still passes. What happens?

Readiness and liveness are independent signals. A pod can be alive but not ready — Kubernetes stops routing traffic to it without restarting anything.
Question 4 of 5

What does the metrics-server provide to the cluster?

metrics-server is the add-on that makes kubectl top and the HorizontalPodAutoscaler possible — it aggregates resource usage from kubelets into an API other components can query.
Question 5 of 5

What does an HPA's targetCPUUtilizationPercentage: 50 control?

The HPA compares actual average CPU utilization across pods against this target and adjusts replica count to move toward it, within the configured min/max bounds.

3. The Concept — Kubernetes That Watches Itself

Blue-green deployments give you a way to switch versions safely, but the switch itself has no opinion about whether what you're switching to is actually working, or whether there's enough of it running to handle the traffic. Probes and autoscaling are what supply those two opinions automatically, without a human watching a dashboard.

Liveness probes: is the container stuck?

A liveness probe checks whether a running container is still functioning — not whether it can serve traffic well, just whether it's alive at all. A common check is a lightweight HTTP endpoint the app exposes for this purpose: 

livenessProbe:
  httpGet:
    path: /healthz
    port: 8080
  periodSeconds: 10
  failureThreshold: 3

Every 10 seconds, the kubelet requests /healthz. After three consecutive failures, it concludes the container is stuck — deadlocked, out of memory, wedged in a bad internal state — and kills and restarts it. This is the mechanism that turns "the app hung" from a page-someone-at-2am incident into something that self-corrects.

Readiness probes: can this pod take a request right now?

A readiness probe asks a narrower question and has a much gentler consequence for failing it: 

readinessProbe:
  httpGet:
    path: /readyz
    port: 8080
  periodSeconds: 5
  failureThreshold: 2

When this fails, the pod is marked Not Ready and the Service stops sending it traffic — but nothing gets restarted. This matters for cases where the container is fine but temporarily shouldn't receive requests: a downstream dependency it needs is briefly unreachable, or it's mid-way through loading a large cache into memory. Restarting wouldn't fix either situation; removing the pod from rotation until it's ready again does.

Startup probes: patience for slow boots

Some applications take a long time to become ready the first time — loading large datasets, warming caches, running migrations. Without a startup probe, a liveness probe with a short periodSeconds can mistake normal slow startup for a hang and restart the container before it ever finishes booting, which can loop forever. 

startupProbe:
  httpGet:
    path: /healthz
    port: 8080
  failureThreshold: 30
  periodSeconds: 5   # up to 150s grace period before liveness takes over

While a startup probe is defined, liveness and readiness checks are suspended until it succeeds once. Only after that does the regular liveness/readiness cycle begin — giving a slow-booting app room to finish starting without being punished for it.

The self-healing feedback loop

Probes and the HorizontalPodAutoscaler solve two different halves of the same problem — one keeps each individual pod honest, the other keeps the right number of pods running — and together they form a loop that runs continuously without anyone watching it:

Neither half of this loop knows the other exists. The liveness probe doesn't know an HPA is running; the HPA doesn't know or care why a given pod is unhealthy. Each does one narrow job continuously, and the combination is what people mean when they call Kubernetes "self-healing" — it isn't one clever mechanism, it's several boring, independent ones running on a loop.

HorizontalPodAutoscaler: matching pod count to load

apiVersion: autoscaling/v2
kind: HorizontalPodAutoscaler
metadata:
  name: myapp-hpa
spec:
  scaleTargetRef:
    apiVersion: apps/v1
    kind: Deployment
    name: myapp
  minReplicas: 1
  maxReplicas: 5
  metrics:
    - type: Resource
      resource:
        name: cpu
        target:
          type: Utilization
          averageUtilization: 50

The HPA controller reconciles roughly every 15 seconds: it reads the current average CPU utilization across the Deployment's pods from the metrics API, compares it to the 50% target, and adjusts replicas up or down — never going below minReplicas or above maxReplicas. Scaling happens gradually, not instantly, to avoid overreacting to a single noisy spike.

4. Lab

Lab objective: Add all three probe types to a Deployment, force a liveness failure and watch Kubernetes restart the pod, force a readiness failure and watch it drop out of traffic without restarting, then apply an HPA and prove it reacts to real CPU load.
1

Add liveness, readiness, and startup probes

containers:
  - name: myapp
    image: myapp:1.0
    ports:
      - containerPort: 8080
    startupProbe:
      httpGet: { path: /healthz, port: 8080 }
      failureThreshold: 30
      periodSeconds: 5
    livenessProbe:
      httpGet: { path: /healthz, port: 8080 }
      periodSeconds: 10
      failureThreshold: 3
    readinessProbe:
      httpGet: { path: /readyz, port: 8080 }
      periodSeconds: 5
      failureThreshold: 2

Apply the updated Deployment and confirm with kubectl get pods that the pod reaches 1/1 Running.

2

Predict, then simulate a liveness failure

Before you do anything: write down what you expect kubectl get pods to show while the liveness probe is failing, and what you expect it to show a minute later.

Exec into the pod and make /healthz start failing (for example, by stopping the process it depends on, or toggling a test flag the app exposes for this purpose). Watch kubectl get pods -w and kubectl describe pod <name> — you should see the restart count increase and an Event describing the liveness failure.

3

Simulate a readiness failure and compare

Now make /readyz fail instead, leaving /healthz passing. Watch kubectl get pods — the pod should show 0/1 in the READY column but stay Running, with no restart count increase. Confirm with kubectl get endpoints myapp-svc that the pod's IP has been removed from the Service.

Stop and think: why is it correct that this pod's restart count doesn't change here, when it did in step 2?

4

Confirm metrics-server is available

Run kubectl top pods. If it errors, install metrics-server for your cluster type before continuing — the HPA cannot function without it. Re-run kubectl top pods until it returns real CPU and memory numbers.

5

Apply the HorizontalPodAutoscaler

Apply an HPA targeting myapp with minReplicas: 1, maxReplicas: 5, and a CPU target of 50%. Confirm with kubectl get hpa — it should show a current CPU percentage and a replica count of 1.

6

Predict, then generate load and watch it scale

Before running the load generator: predict roughly how many replicas you expect once CPU stays above 50% for a sustained period.

Run a small pod that loops wget requests against the service continuously, and watch kubectl get hpa -w in a second terminal. You should see the CPU percentage climb and REPLICAS increase over the next couple of reconcile cycles, up to but not past maxReplicas.

7

Stop the load and watch it scale back down

Delete the load generator pod. Keep watching kubectl get hpa -w — CPU usage should fall, and after a cooldown period the HPA should reduce replicas back toward minReplicas. Note roughly how long the scale-down took compared to how fast the scale-up happened.

8. Post-Coding Quiz

Question 1 of 5

A pod shows 1/1 Running but 0/1 ready. What's the most likely explanation?

Running plus not-ready is exactly the readiness-without-liveness-failure case from step 3 of the lab — the container is fine, it just isn't receiving traffic right now.
Question 2 of 5

Why give a slow-starting app its own startup probe instead of just relaxing the liveness probe's timing?

Loosening the liveness probe's own thresholds would make it slower to catch a real post-startup hang too. A separate startup probe keeps that fast liveness response while still tolerating a slow boot.
Question 3 of 5

What typically causes the HPA to scale a Deployment back down?

The HPA continuously compares current utilization to its target; once usage has been below target for long enough, it reduces replicas — the same mechanism that scaled up, running in reverse.
Question 4 of 5

Why is "self-healing" in Kubernetes usually the combination of probes and an HPA, rather than one single feature?

Probes keep individual pods honest; the HPA keeps enough of them running. Neither component is aware the other exists — the "self-healing" behavior emerges from both running continuously at once.
Question 5 of 5

Session 13 builds on the same "verify before it gets full traffic" idea from blue-green and probes, but shifts a percentage of traffic at a time instead of switching all at once.

9. Reflection Questions

  1. Before simulating the liveness and readiness failures, did you correctly predict which one would trigger a restart? What was your reasoning?
  2. Why might it be dangerous to point a liveness probe at an endpoint that itself depends on a slow external service?
  3. If your app's normal CPU usage is spiky by nature (bursts every few minutes), how might that affect your choice of HPA target percentage and min/max replicas?

10. What Breaks If This Knowledge Is Missing?

  • No liveness probe: a container that deadlocks or wedges internally just sits there forever, reported as Running, silently serving nothing or serving errors, until a human notices and restarts it manually.
  • No readiness probe: a pod that's still warming up (or briefly can't reach a dependency) keeps receiving live traffic anyway, since Kubernetes has no signal telling it not to route requests there.
  • Liveness probe pointed at the wrong endpoint, or with no startup probe for a slow app: the container gets killed and restarted repeatedly during normal startup, a self-inflicted crash loop that looks like an outage but is actually the probe configuration itself.
  • No HPA: a fixed replica count means either overpaying for capacity that sits idle most of the time, or under-provisioning and getting overwhelmed the moment real traffic arrives.

11. What We Learned

Today you learned: liveness probes restart stuck containers, readiness probes gate traffic without restarting anything, startup probes give slow-booting apps a grace period, and a HorizontalPodAutoscaler reads metrics-server data to keep pod count matched to real CPU load — together forming the self-healing loop that makes it safe to trust an environment before flipping traffic to it.
